Great question, and one I often ask in interviews. Here's my framework for prioritization, which my product managers tend to follow (more or less):

  1. Prioritize themes, not projects - Create a list of themes for your product or business. Examples might be customer acquisition, activation, retention, avg revenue per user, avg visits per user, etc. Pick ~3 that are the most important for your product given its stage. If your churn rate is extremely low then you don't have much to gain by focusing on retention, so you should probably focus elsewhere. If you get gobs of traffic but few stick then you shouldn't focus on driving more traffic, you should focus on activation.
  2. Sequence and resource your themes - Of the three themes you're going to focus on (for now), prioritize them and try to come up with an idea of approximately how many of your resources you're going to allocate to each.
  3. Generate project ideas - Focus your energy on coming up with great project ideas related to your 3 themes. Start with what you have already, but work to come up with more, bigger ideas. Try to break larger projects into chunks, and focus on the first 20% of the project that's likely to generate 80% of the project's impact (cherry-pick).
  4. Estimate each project's potential impact - Assess how much leverage each project has on the relevant theme. An extra click upstream (e.g. on a search result) is worth 10-100 clicks downstream. Based on this, do a quick forecast of the impact of the project. Simply try to get within an order of magnitude.
  5. Estimate each project's cost - Get the right people in the room to roll each project idea around and SWAG a cost in resources, $, and other scarce resources.
  6. Prioritize projects within each theme - Look for the projects with the greatest projected impact with the least cost, and do these ones first, quickly. Then move on to the next projects, or the next phases of the early projects that had a greater than expected impact.


This framework has a couple benefits:

  • It forces you to really think through the most important themes for your product or business. It helps you create a plan, rather than just a list of good ideas.
  • It forces you to allocate resources to themes explicitly, not haphazardly, and prevents you from spending time on "the thick of thin things".
  • It eliminates the need to prioritize very different projects against each other. You no longer have to make a call whether reducing 500ms of latency is more important than gaining 5K new users.
  • It forces you to model/forecast impact of projects. Doing this, measuring actual impact, and then comparing the two is the only way to really improve your forecasting ability and internalize the learnings (often called product management instincts or "spidey sense")

I really like Ian's answer. An alternative, quick and dirty way to do this with a small startup team that's heads down and buried in development is the following

  1. Identify your 3 or 4 core business goals for the next 3/6/9 months. If you have a board or investors settle this at a board meeting. If you don't yet have investors, settle it among the co-founders. Your goals might be: to prove product viability, to prove customer economics, to prove product desirability, customer acquisition, etc. So: get really solid on what you're trying to achieve in the next few quarters.

    Edit: Andy Pipes left a comment that has since been deleted that suggested that prioritizing based just on "business priority" was a recipe for disaster since you'd end up with a product that no one likes except for the board. It's a great point. I have assumed that your business goals in #1 are informed by customer feedback, the usage and data you're seeing, etc. Your priorities

    obviously (I hope) should not be made in a vacuum without regard to the user.
  2. Then take all of your features, the recommendations, the suggestions, the backlog, etc. and get them in a spreadsheet in one column. Then put each of the business priorities in separate columns. Give each feature or item on the list a 1 under the business priority it maps to. If it doesn't map to a priority, put a zero.
  3. You'll quickly find that there are only a few that drive more than one business priority and plenty that in fact don't really drive any.
  4. Take all those that don't map to a business priority and shelve them for now.
  5. Take those that do map to business priorities and force rank those. At the same time it's helpful to get a ballpark idea of the size of the project (e.g. is it bigger than a breadbox, etc.)
  6. Map out the next 6 months based on those priorities and your resources available. The next 3 months should have a confidence of ~90%, the next 3 ~65-75% and after that it goes down to like 40% confidence because your roadmap will evolve during that time.
  7. Get planning, if you're agile think about your sprint schedule, etc. And absolutely do user testing to test your assumptions and theories. See if product features or new designs will resonate before you start building. A few thousand dollars in research can save you a ton of wasted opportunity by developing something that no one wants.

Let me start off by saying that feature prioritization is a very different problem across different types of products and industries. The way you prioritize features for products that are physically manufactured and have distribution costs is different than enterprise software, and they are both different than the way you prioritize for consumer software or consumer internet products.

I wrote a blog post on this in 2009 specifically on the topic of prioritizing features for consumer internet products:
http://blog.adamnash.com/2009/07/22/guide-to-product-planning-three-feature-buckets/

I believe that there are at least three distinct buckets of feature enhancements, and most consumer product managers get into trouble when they confuse them.

  • Metrics Movers. These are the features that "pay the bills". In the end, if your product isn't successful, you won't have the time or resources to continue development. Success has a definition for every product, and a good product manager has clearly defined metrics for their features. If you fail to prioritize metrics movers, then in the end, your products generally fail to meet their goals, and eventually lose funding / support.
  • Customer Requests. These are features that have been requested directly by your customers. They are typically incremental enhancements. If you fail to prioritize customer requests, you run the risk of alienating your customers and missing key insights based on actual use cases.
  • Delight. This is the bucket for innovative features that are internally generated based on insights in design or technology to enable new exciting capabilities or an innovative new user experience. If you fail to prioritize delight features, you end up with mundane, functional products that fail to inspire loyalty or passion in your users.


While there are features that can meet the definition of more than one of these buckets, it's actually quite common for individual features to only fall in one of these areas. Well balanced major product releases typically have features that represent all three of these buckets, so it's crucial for product managers to properly identify the primary motivation for each minor feature.

During those crucial times when features are on the chopping block due to time, resource, or other constraints, knowing which features will move the metrics vs. respond to customers vs. inspire delight can be the difference between success & failure.

Since writing this blog post, I've added a fourth bucket, which reflects some of the learnings that have influenced prioritization at LinkedIn:

  • Strategic. There are times when you include features that meet the definition of none of the previous three buckets, but are included for strategic reasons. These reasons can include experimentation (e.g. how will users interact with or utilize this feature) or data (e.g. we need to start the user-generated content funnel now so that months from now we can build a higher level feature over it).


Great product managers are always learning from their releases, and including strategic features to enable learning or future capabilities definitely has a role to play in product feature prioritization as well.
