What 8 Years as a Marine Taught Me About Being a Leader

Tags: leadership

Bookmark ID: 876770580

For the first eight years of my career I was in the U.S. Marines.

That means that I got my foundational education in what it means to be a leader from the military.

Your mind probably jumps to the common stereotypes about military life, guessing that those years were marked by command and control, no-nonsense, “drop and give me 20” examples of leadership. But that wasn’t my experience at all (Okay, maybe a bit in boot camp). In reality my days in the military taught that a leader’s job is to support and empower their people.

It turns out that the military is very good at developing “human capital literacy” in leaders — having a deep understanding of your people, their skills and experiences, and what they bring to your organization.

When I left the military and entered the corporate world, I saw some big opportunities to apply the leadership lessons I learned as a Marine to managing teams in any organization. Here’s what I learned that we can all apply in our work.

Eat Last

You’ve probably heard this one before. “Leaders eat last” is the idea that true leaders put their employees first. In the military it’s more than a catchphrase; it’s a reality.

The highest-ranking person is always the last to eat. As a brand-new Marine, often my commander or platoon leader would actually serve us, passing out MREs or serving food at the chow hall to me and my fellow Marines. They made sure we had time to eat and a moment to relax. Sometimes that meant they wouldn’t get a meal for themselves until a couple of hours later because they had a battalion of 600 Marines to take care of.

The longer I was in the military, the more I understood why leaders eat last. There is no way any leader in the military -or any organization- can complete their mission alone: they need the strength and unique skills of their team. Simply put, a leader’s goal is to equip people with the tools and resources they need to be successful. It’s to protect and empower them so that together, the group can reach a shared goal — sometimes a goal as basic as “don’t get blown up by the enemy.” If that’s your goal, you want your people to be fed.

When I came to the corporate world I brought this approach with me. I constantly ask what resources everyone on my team needs to be successful. How can I help them? How can I make sure I’m eating last?

Start Training Your Replacements Immediately

As a young leader, the most important lesson I heard in every training was “On Day One, look around to see who is going to take over your job.” Marines have a vested interest in preparing their succession plan. The stakes are higher, but that mentality puts a focus on developing other people instead of relying only on your own skills or experience.

One way to train your people well? Get really clear about what you do. Every time I showed up to a new job or moved into a new role, my new boss would clearly explain the unit’s mission, his or her role, and my role. That sounds like an obvious thing to do on someone’s first day. But in the corporate world, we often assume that new hires already know the basics. We don’t explain the context upfront, and people eventually learn it by osmosis. How much more empowering would it be to clearly, explicitly explain roles, functions and your core mission upfront?

Make Time to Practice

Here’s something I really valued about being in the Marines: We constantly practiced. In the business world all decisions are real-world decisions, which can make it hard to give junior people big, challenging tasks. You don’t want them to mess up, right?

But in the Marines, we spent most of our time in practice mode. Before I deployed to Afghanistan, I spent weeks on practice patrols. Actors played potential insurgents. We were surprised by mock attacks. And we all got the chance to play the role of squad leader.

When you make a junior marine the squad leader in a demo, obviously they’re going to make missteps and fail. But it's such a valuable lesson for junior Marines to be in their leader’s shoes, and failure is a great educator. They see how challenging it is, they see the split-second decisions they have to make and they learn how important it is for a team to listen to its leader. When they’re thrown into a command role, they immediately see what they'll need to learn from their leader in order to one day do lead successfully.

This type of opportunity is rarely given to staff in the corporate world. Team members are rarely given the opportunity to make decisions, to fail, and thus they miss the opportunity to learn.

I try to create as many “practice” opportunities as I can and to give junior people responsibility early on. Let them make those missteps and keep practicing so one day when they take over they’ll have experience (and scars) to help them succeed.

Respect Everyone

There’s no doubt that rank is important in the military. If you’re addressing someone who is higher up the chain of command than you, you always say the person’s rank and “Sir” or “Ma’am.” But I was surprised to find that good leaders extended that respect both ways. My leaders showed deference down the chain of command too. Even though leaders senior to me didn’t have to call me by my rank, they treated me with the same respect I showed them. I was “Sergeant Jackson.”

Even in flatter corporate hierarchies, we can learn a lot about respecting our peers, leaders and especially the people we lead.

At Sift, I’m trying to model the examples of leadership I saw in the Marines. I’m arming people with the resources they need to be successful, training everyone for their next job, building an iterative “practice” culture where it’s OK to fail and infusing everything we do with respect for each other — no ego allowed.
