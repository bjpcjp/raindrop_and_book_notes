Mark Twain once said that the two most important days in your life are the day you are born, and the day that you discover why. My goal for writing this article is to share an approach to discover (or in my case, rediscover) your “why”.

My transition

This past January, I stepped down from the job of a lifetime - serving as Intuit’s fifth CEO, a job I had the privilege of holding for 11 amazing years. While it was the job of a lifetime, I knew it could not be a job for a lifetime. Like all chapters in our lives, I knew the end date was inevitable and my goal was simply to be a part of making that decision!

I had carefully planned the milestones that would help me determine my exit date years in advance and partnered closely with our board and the incoming CEO to ensure a smooth succession. As I wrapped up my final days in December 2018, I was confident that I had a plan for what I would do once I handed over the reins. I would invest my time serving on boards, coaching young executives and spending more time with my family.

On Jan 1st, 2019 with that clarity of focus, my muscle memory kicked in. I got out of bed and hit the gym at 5:30 am like I always did. I read the WSJ, watched the Opening Bell on CNBC and headed to my new office as Executive Chairman of Intuit. From there, I filled my days with meetings until 7:00 pm. I was just as busy as I had been as CEO, but something felt off balance.

By March, I was beginning to feel rudderless and lost. To be clear, I wasn’t regretting my decision to step down. In fact, I was more convinced than ever that it was the right decision. For me, the growing struggle was that for more than a decade, I had gotten out of bed to advance Intuit’s mission. Now I found myself feeling uncertain about what the mission for my next chapter should be.

My mission quest

I decided to answer this question by applying a technique we practice at Intuit on a daily basis – seek to learn from the best wheel makers. I set out to interview others who had navigated a similar transition, individuals who were further along their path in life and had successfully crafted a new chapter. I talked to athletes, business leaders, academic leaders, and elected officials, all who had an impact and demonstrated incredible vitality in the next stage of their life. I met with dozens of individuals and sought to understand the choices they made in their earlier years that led them to fulfilled lives at this stage in their journey.

The consistency of their answers was startling. Simply stated, each had taken the time to discover their “why”. In their own words, they explained that how one chooses to spend their days is simply a means to an end. The question I needed to answer was whether I was being purposeful about what I wanted it to add up to? Was I clear why it all mattered?

How to (re)discover your why

What emerged from these interviews was a series of questions that I have translated into a blueprint for how to discover your why.

Before illustrating how this blueprint works, let me begin by explaining what it’s not. Like most of you, I’ve developed a foundational understanding of my personal strengths and weaknesses over the years. We’ve all had coaches or managers, watched game film or had performance reviews, benefited from 360-degree feedback, taken performance or personality tests, and engaged in lots of ways to help us better understand our gifts and shadows. But as these wise mentors pointed out, knowing your strengths and weaknesses is not the answer.

To discover your “why”, they explained that I needed to reflect back on my life and look for patterns that answered four questions:

  1. What things did I regret having sacrificed to-date that I now wish to prioritize higher in my life going forward?
  2. When in my life (and not simply my career) have I found myself performing at peak levels, filled with genuine passion and purpose?
  3. In what environments do I seem to perform at my best?
  4. In what environments do I not perform at peak levels or enjoy the work?

Armed with these questions, my work began. I listed the things I regretted not having invested enough time doing in my hyper-focused all-consuming career. This included developing a shared mission with my wife (beyond our home and our children) that we could build together. I also wanted to spend more time with our daughters, our mothers and our siblings who live in other parts of the country.

I then reflected on the times in my life when I tended to perform at peak levels, filled with genuine passion and purpose. This exercise was harder, but as I took the time to write things down, patterns emerged. Going back to my childhood, I remembered loving my responsibility as a safety patrol guard in elementary school; as a lifeguard in the youth-end of the pool; as a martial arts instructor teaching individuals and young children self-confidence and self-defense; as an ally to a friend who had the courage to come out as gay; and when I summoned the courage to face a bully in the 9th grade.

A pattern was clearly emerging which carried over into my career. The jobs I found most fulfilling were when I was serving those who were fighting against the odds - individuals and families struggling to make it paycheck to paycheck, small businesses and self-employed who dare to dream despite knowing that 50% will fail within five years, and serving geographic regions trying desperately to reinvent their communities & create a better tomorrow.

My North Star was clear - I’ve spent a lifetime fighting for the underdog and trying to tilt the odds in their favor.

I then answered the third question - in which environments I seem to be at my best. I love tackling seemingly intractable problems that require innovation, reinvention and transformational change. I love doing it with a team, and I love coaching - helping to translate individual ability into organizational capability. I’ve always believed that if your goals can be achieved alone, you aren’t dreaming big enough.

Finally, I answered the fourth question – in what environments I am not at my best. I’ve always struggled in environments that are too opinion-based and philosophical, overly procedural and slow-moving, or when the mission is more about making money than it is about some higher purpose. I also struggle in roles that lack structure or an ability to measure tangible progress.

The result of my 90-day journey

The process was illuminating and enabled me to (re)discover my why.

 “I seek to be a champion for the overlooked and underserved, to be a warrior for human dignity and human potential, and to level the playing field of opportunity for all who possess the aspiration and the grit to try.”

In partnership with my wife, we’re forming the “One Wing Foundation” in reference to our favorite quote - “we are each of us angels with only one wing, and we can only fly by embracing one another”. Our initial focus will be to advance what we see as the two great equalizers … education and entrepreneurship … in regions where individuals and communities have lost hope.

The challenge we’ve chosen to tackle is what we call the 75% reality. 75% of Gen Z now dream of owning their own business and 75% of all new jobs will be created in start-ups, but 75% of venture investments last year went to 3 states – California, New York and Massachusetts. Aspiration and talent are equally distributed, but opportunity is not, and our One Wing Foundation will seek to change that reality!

In addition to working with my wife through the One Wing Foundation, I’ll continue to serve as the Executive Chairman for Intuit, which includes being actively involved in the company’s Corporate Social Responsibility efforts. This includes partnering with thought-leaders on a project called “Start-Up West Virginia”, modeled after countries and cities who have faced similar issues - shifting industrial bases, shrinking populations, painful addictions and the exodus of their youth – but have successfully solved these challenges and risen from the ashes to create a better tomorrow.

Finally, I’m assisting my Alma Mater – Marshall University – to re-imagine the business school by adopting the entrepreneurial techniques of the Silicon Valley to better prepare students for the 21st century. This effort is supported by scholarships from our One Wing Foundation for students from West Virginia and Ohio who are the first in their families to attend college.

The wisdom learned from these amazing individuals and the blueprint that emerged revealed a clarity of purpose that was both needed and energizing in ways I never could have imagined.

Testing the end result

A few weeks ago, I had dinner with a good friend who serves as the CEO of a successful technology company in the Silicon Valley. He patiently listened as I explained my journey and the insight I had gained. When I finished, he stated with conviction that the highest impact way for me to fulfill my “why” would be to become the CEO of a public company once again. He suggested that it would give me a bigger platform to affect change.

Hearing his POV was unsettling. I had just stepped down from the best public company CEO role in the world (in my opinion), and I was being told that it may have been the best way to fulfill my “why” after all.

The next morning, I sat at my desk and stared at my copy of the Trillion Dollar Coach – the recently published book that outlines Bill Campbell’s leadership playbook. Bill had been Intuit’s Chairman and was my coach and mentor for more than a dozen years before he passed away in 2016. I still miss his presence every day.

It was at that moment that it hit me. Bill Campbell didn’t become the Trillion Dollar Coach because he was associated with Intuit, Apple, Google, Chegg or any of the other companies who can claim him as their own. To the contrary, those companies and their leaders became who they were because of Bill Campbell. Bill didn’t need a platform to make the impact he did. Bill became the platform.

Over the years, Bill and I shared a passion for stories and in particular, for the legend of King Arthur. Arthur was a leader who led through serving others, with a round table of equals bonded by a higher purpose, guided by the lesson they discovered when they lost their way and got back on track through their quest for the Holy Grail. The lesson they learned was profound – “that which you seek cannot be found, it must be made, the land and the king are one.” Bill and I interpreted this to mean that you should “strive to be the change that you seek in this world”.

At the moment of truth, Bill was with me once again, reminding me to be the change that I seek in this world. My doubts evaporated. My confidence was strengthened in what I seek to achieve, and I was assured that I didn’t need to be a CEO to do it!

My hope in writing this article is that my journey will help you in some small way, and for those who have yet to do so, to discover your “why”. Be the platform for the change that you seek in the world, and regardless of the role you occupy in life … go put your dent in the Universe!
