The amazing rise — and shocking fall — of Indiana's caviar king

This is a whopper of a fish story. But it’s true. It’s a wild tale with a little bit of everything: A pillar of the community sent to prison. A prehistoric fish. An undercover sting. A fledgling Indiana industry fueled by ecological strife halfway around the globe. A U.S. Supreme Court ruling. Money. Guns. And tons of Hoosier caviar. 

A grisly scene surfaced on the Ohio River in the southeastern corner of Indiana in the spring of 1997.

Hundreds of eviscerated paddlefish carcasses bobbed in the muddy water near Markland Locks and Dam. Their bellies slashed open. The eggs ripped out.

It was a wake-up call for wildlife biologists and conservation officers who manage the state’s Ohio River fishery — greed was rampant on the river.

Over-fishing in the Caspian Sea had devastated the beluga sturgeon fishery, which drove demand for caviar from other sources. The American paddlefish, an odd-looking prehistoric creature found in the Ohio River, offered a high-quality substitute.

The slaughter at Markland Dam highlighted a new reality on the Ohio River: The booming caviar market was beginning to pose a threat to the long-term survival of a fish that has been around for 400 million years. It also helped set off a series of investigations into illegal fishing.

Poaching "is a perpetual problem when it comes to caviar production," said Tina Shaw, a spokeswoman for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

"I can tell you," Shaw explained, "that greed is a good motivator."

Twenty years after the Markland Dam incident, David Cox, the caviar king of Indiana, found himself in the cross-hairs of one of those poaching investigations — trapped in a net like the paddlefish he pulled from the river. His problem started after he hired an eager young man.

Offering a job to a stranger wasn’t out of character for Cox in 2017. Years before, Cox also had hired a friend's nephew after he was released from prison.

“Without knowing him personally, Dave took him in, gave him food, training, a job, transportation, and a place to live," the friend would later write. "But more than that, he gave him trust and a chance … It was then that I discovered that Dave did this for many struggling addicts and others who were just in unfortunate and desperate situations.”

This time, Cox’s generosity — and, possibly, greed — appears to have backfired.

The man he invited onto his boat was an undercover conservation officer. He was recording their conversations. At one point, the officer asked Cox why the paddlefish population was declining.

“That’s because I killed them all,” Cox boasted.

'Black Gold' rush on the river

The American paddlefish, Polyodon spathula, is a prehistoric behemoth from the same group of ﬁsh as sturgeon. They’re called Acipenseriforms, and their roe is prized as caviar.

With lifespans of up to 30 years, the odd-looking fish with a prominent paddle-shaped snout can grow to 10 feet long and weigh as much as 200 pounds. The oldest fossil records of paddlefish date back about 400 million years — well before the age of dinosaurs, according to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

Native to the Mississippi River basin, paddlefish can be found today in rivers and lakes in more than 20 states. That habitat includes the Ohio and Wabash rivers.

Paddlefish, according to the USFWS website, once was "the most important commercial species in the Mississippi River Valley." They were sought after for both their flesh, and the roe from mature females. But their numbers fell sharply as commercial fishing peaked around 1900.

Since then, paddlefish in the Ohio River have gone through what wildlife experts describe as boom-and-bust cycles. The population swings are marked by periods of over-fishing and then, as the number of fish dwindles, a decline in fishing due to unprofitability. The down periods help the paddlefish population recover, but it sometimes takes decades.

In the middle part of the 20th century, the fishery was in a recovery cycle. By the 1980s, though, growing demand for paddlefish caviar brought on another surge of fishing.

It didn’t take much to cash in on the new roe rush. Basically, all someone needed was a boat and a few hundred bucks for nets. The investment paled in contrast to the potential payoff. The eggs from one mature female could fetch $800 to $1,000 for a fisherman.

High roe prices provided an incentive to haul in more fish — including a growing number being caught illegally.

New border, new industry

The new market for paddlefish roe emerged around the same time the U.S. Supreme Court took up a legal fight between Indiana and Kentucky over their shared border along the Ohio River.

Up until that time, Kentucky had controlled the entire river. But the country’s highest court ruled the boundary between the two states should be the low-water mark on the Indiana shore in 1792, when Kentucky became a state.

Dams and course changes in the river submerged that line in some places, and made it difficult to determine in others. The court let the states sort out the details.

A deal struck in October 1981 handed Indiana control from a minimum of 100 feet from the present northern shoreline and more than 500 feet in some places. Indiana suddenly had to develop regulations for fishing on the Ohio.

The Indiana Department of Natural Resources chose policies that aligned with those of Kentucky. Rules for commercial fishing, including the harvest of paddlefish, took effect in 1988.

Initially, DNR officials knew little about the state’s paddlefish population. That started to change after they joined counterparts from six other states bordering the river to create the Ohio River Fisheries Management Team and began conducting joint research in 1995.

Still, it was hard to measure the impact Indiana’s new commercial fishermen were having on paddlefish. There wasn’t the manpower to closely monitor the 358 miles of river delineating Indiana’s southern border. That meant the fledgling Hoosier industry remained basically a free-for-all.

A report from the multi-state fisheries team noted "rampant illegal and questionable fishery activity related to paddlefish taking in Indiana."

Record catch and a big bust

Several incidents, including the 1997 slaughter at Markland Dam, prompted more scrutiny and oversight. It was a critical time.

The DNR responded with emergency rule changes in 1998. Among them: a ban on fishing near dams and the mouths of tributaries, where paddlefish often congregate to feed and spawn.

Commercial fishermen also were required, for the first time, to file monthly reports detailing their take of paddlefish meat and eggs. Yet even then, the tracking was based on self-reporting and an honor system.

For the Indiana caviar industry, 2007 was a seminal year.

Indiana’s commercial fishermen had aggressively pursued female paddlefish plump with roe through the early 2000s, peaking with a harvest of 10,420 pounds in 2006. One of those fishermen was David Cox, a heavily tattooed high school dropout who’d spent most of his life hunting and fishing along the Ohio River. Cox was now a born-again Christian, baptized in the river he loved.

The next year, the harvest began to decline.

That year, an 18-month undercover investigation culminated in the arrests of more than 20 people on dozens of charges related to the illegal harvest of paddlefish. Dubbed Operation Skid Roe, the probe by state and federal officials broke up what the DNR termed an illegal poaching operation.

Some of those involved, the DNR reported, hauled in $100,000 to $400,000 a year — one fat fish at a time.

Beginning of the end

Cox was not among those charged in the 2007 bust.

But fallout from that investigation did, in a roundabout way, lead to Cox’s downfall 10 years later. By that time, the website for Cox's business boasted of his central role in the industry: "For the past few years," it says, "MIDWEST CAVIAR has caught half of the harvest reported being caught in Kentucky and Indiana."

Months after the initial splash of Operation Skid Roe — on Aug. 1, 2007 — Cox and his wife, Vicki, incorporated Midwest Caviar LLC, to buy roe and process it into finished caviar.

Turning a mass of paddlefish eggs into caviar is relatively easy. First, the eggs are removed from the fish and separated from the skein, a membrane that binds them. Then they're washed and lightly salted.

Cox and his wife ran their caviar business out of a small metal building adjacent to their home. It was surrounded by ponds stocked with giant catfish Cox pulled from the river. A sign by one door said "Let’s Kill Fish" in big red letters.

As the Coxes launched the new business, their son-in-law, Joseph Schigur, found himself in a tough spot. Indiana put a limit on commercial fishing licenses. Schigur was unable to obtain one. He also applied for a non-resident Kentucky license in 2007, but he had no luck there, either.

Schigur applied for a Kentucky license again in 2008. This time he claimed to be a resident. After providing a bogus address in Owensboro, Schigur obtained a Kentucky license.

That new license was critical to Schigur and his wife, Jessica, the daughter of David and Vicki Cox. In 2009, the Schigurs incorporated Ohio Valley Caviar LLC, a processing business like the one operated by the Coxes.

Now the town of English, a community of 629 in Indiana's poorest county, was the caviar capitol of Indiana.

Tons of caviar — and cash 

Schigur's work on the river caught the attention of federal wildlife officials in 2011.

His wife had a non-resident Kentucky roe-buyer permit. And she had purchased more than two tons of raw paddlefish eggs from Kentucky-licensed fishermen in 2010 and 2011. During that same period, her husband had continued fishing with a license that claimed he lived in Kentucky.

When Schigur was confronted about his Kentucky resident license, court records say he admitted living in Indiana. But he continued fishing. Kentucky records show he sold his wife more than 1,600 pounds of roe caught on his Kentucky license in 2012 — a haul that federal authorities said was valued at close to a quarter-million dollars.

Indiana officials took even more steps in 2012 to oversee commercial fishing.

For the first time, the DNR began issuing separate roe-harvest and roe-buyer licenses. Under the new process, Indiana harvesters could only sell their paddlefish roe to buyers licensed by Indiana.

The DNR also required buyers and sellers to file monthly reports detailing their daily catches and purchases. The new, detailed state reports did not cover the heyday of Indiana's caviar industry, encompassing less than 10 percent of the 46 tons of caviar harvested by Indiana-licensed fishermen since 1999. That makes it impossible to calculate Cox's total caviar haul.

The new reporting procedures coincided with a dramatic drop in roe harvest by Indiana fishermen.

Benson, the DNR spokesman, said a number of factors played into the decline. He said the new licensing fees and reporting requirements likely deterred some fishermen, as did an increased law enforcement presence on the river.

Pressure from fishing also was driving the paddlefish population toward another "bust" cycle.

"We have heard directly from multiple commercial anglers," Benson told IndyStar, "that the paddlefish population — especially the number of large, gravid females — has really declined over the past several years."

Benson said fishermen also reported an increase in caviar from farm-raised fish in China and other sources was depressing prices.

Still, records obtained by IndyStar reveal Cox and his wife remained busy from 2012 through 2017. David or Vicki Cox reported buying about 4,250 pounds of eggs from Indiana roe harvesters.

Joseph Schigur also reported buying about 250 pounds of roe from Indiana fishermen in 2012 and 2013.

Those tallies, however, reflect only a fraction of the roe that passed through Ohio Valley Caviar and Midwest Caviar.

From 2010-2017, the Schigurs caught or bought more than 20,000 pounds of Kentucky caviar, while the Coxes caught or bought nearly 5,000 pounds.

Combined, nearly 15 tons of caviar passed through the two processing plants in English from 2010-2017. The wholesale value of the caviar was more than $4 million — and $12 million or more on the retail market.

But you couldn't buy any of the hometown delicacy at the local Jay C Food store — or anywhere else in English.

DNR investigates son-in-law

State and federal officials launched yet another investigation in 2014 following "reoccurring complaints" into commercial fishing on the Ohio and Wabash rivers.

"Because there is considerable money in the caviar business, there is an incentive for commercial anglers to bend or break the rules," Benson, the DNR spokesman, explained.

During the 2014 investigation, court records say "Joseph Schigur’s commercial American paddlefish operation, Ohio Valley Caviar, came under increased scrutiny for potential violations of state commercial fishing operations."

The following year, as the paddlefish season reached its springtime peak, Schigur was looking for a fishing assistant. He hired an eager young man who'd approached him about a job. The new helper worked with Schigur for nearly a year.

At one point, Schigur's assistant learned his boss wanted to ship caviar to the East Coast. But he needed a trucker to transport it. The helper recommended a friend. Schigur hired him, and the driver made multiple trips to New York with a refrigerated truck full of smoked paddlefish meat and caviar.

During five trips to New York in late 2015 and early 2016, which are detailed in court records, the truck driver delivered more than 700 pounds of caviar valued at about $100,000. He'd then return to English with thousands of dollars in cash and checks.

It wasn't until much later that Schigur learned the helper, and truck driver, weren't what they appeared. Both were undercover conservation officers.

Schigur pleaded guilty in 2016 to a federal misdemeanor for lying about his address on the Kentucky license applications. He was sentenced to one year of probation and ordered to pay a $5,000 fine — not much more than the haul from one good day on the river. Authorities also confiscated nearly 1,000 pounds of caviar. 

Neither Schigur nor his attorney responded to requests for comment from IndyStar. Vicki Cox said her daughter and son-in-law were advised by their attorney not to speak with the media.

Focus shifts to Cox

Investigators turned their attention to Cox after he hired the same undercover officer who'd worked with Schigur.

Court records say the officer secretly made video and audio recordings that captured many of his interactions with Cox as they fished together on the Ohio River in early 2017.

Cox and the undercover officer fished in Indiana and Kentucky waters, catching or attempting to harvest paddlefish in violation of state laws, according to court records. The alleged violations included fishing too close to the mouths of streams and dams. Cox also was accused of using an oversized needle to puncture the bellies of fish to check for eggs and failing to properly document his catches.

The investigation came to a head April 17, 2017. It was a busy day on the river for Cox and his new helper.

Court records say Cox pulled an undersized female paddlefish from a net he’d set in Kentucky water. Regulations in both states prohibit keeping a fish less than 32 inches long, measured from the eye to the fork in its tail. It is also illegal to remove roe from fish while on the water or shoreline.

The fish, according to prosecutors, "measured, at most, 31.5” in length."

What happened next is a point of contention.

As they motored back into Indiana water in Cox’s 24-foot Alweld aluminum boat, prosecutors claim Cox slowed the boat. He looked around in all directions. Then with the boat still creeping forward, Cox "grabbed a filet knife, cut the paddlefish open, and cut the eggs out of the fish." He quickly threw the gutted carcass overboard.

Put the eggs in a plastic bag and hide it, Cox instructed the undercover officer. Loading the boat onto a trailer, Cox urged his helper to hurry. They needed to get out of the area, he said, "before they got a ticket for the illegal fish."

Cox later told Louisville TV station WDRB 41 that he did cut open the fish and removed its eggs while still on the water. But he said the undercover officer pushed him to do it.

"I said throw the fish back," Cox told the TV station, "and time and time again he said, 'Let's just take them out. Let's just take them out.'"

It wasn’t Cox’s only crime that day, according to prosecutors.

He'd also offered to sell a semi-automatic handgun to the undercover officer in a transaction secretly recorded.

That was a big mistake. Cox had a prior felony conviction for illegally transporting a bear from Tennessee to Indiana in 1990. He was legally prohibited from possessing firearms.

A raid in the town of English

The sun was just rising on May 10, 2017, when dozens of officers swarmed Cox’s home and adjacent caviar facility. Guns drawn, they called out his name.

"They was hollering, 'David! David!'" Cox told WDBR 41 after the raid. "And there was as many as you could possibly put on my stairway with assault rifles and down here on the ground pointing them at me."

Another law enforcement team hit the home and business of Cox’s daughter and son-in-law.

Before the day was over, wildlife officers seized about 400 pounds of caviar, $20,000 in cash and checks, a boat, a truck and fishing records from Cox’s home and business.

They also confiscated 11 guns.

At Ohio Valley Caviar, the business owned and operated by Cox’s daughter and son-in-law, officers packed up another 982 pounds of caviar and nearly a ton of paddlefish meat. They also seized a boat and trailer.

The nearly 1,400 pounds of caviar seized in the raids had an estimated wholesale value of $200,000.

No one was arrested that day. But a harsh reckoning was coming.

A palpable distaste for the law  

More than a year passed after the raids before prosecutors leveled charges against Cox in U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Indiana.

The federal case filed Aug. 3 alleged Cox illegally possessed a firearm and violated the Lacey Act. That latter charge related to the undersized paddlefish, but prosecutors said it didn’t reflect the full scope of his fishing violations.

"The defendant’s disregard of commercial fishing regulations extended beyond simply keeping and killing an undersized paddlefish," prosecutors noted. "He fished in closed areas where paddlefish congregate, contributing to overfishing the species. He underreported the fish that he caught or that died in his nets, affecting biologists’ ability to accurately assess the health of the species. And he used a crude, oversized hollow metal rod to stab the fish and check if they were bearing eggs, which can injure or kill the fish."

The criminal allegations shocked many in English, where Cox had a reputation as a devout Christian and good neighbor. Supporters wrote dozens of letters urging U.S. District Judge Tanya Walton Pratt to be lenient at Cox’s sentencing.

One of those letters came from Crawford County Sheriff Jeff Howell. He described Cox as a friend.

“I have seen David in his good times and bad times but know that the good far surpasses the bad,” Howell wrote. “David has shown over and over the heart to help others without being asked or needing to be recognized for it.”

The sheriff also vouched for Cox’s character in the letter dated Aug. 30.

But just weeks before Cox was to be sentenced, the sheriff's office got a call. It was from an ammunition dealer. The man knew about the federal charges against Cox. And he was concerned about a recent request.

Cox had asked about buying 600 rounds of assault-rifle ammunition “for a friend.”

Prosecutors cited the incident as another reason Cox should go to prison. His distaste for the law, they wrote in a sentencing memorandum, "is palpable."

"He even told (the undercover conservation officer) that he has a tattoo on his leg of a game warden hanging from a tree," they wrote. "He is proud of his prior felony assault on a federal officer. He has consistently flouted wildlife laws. He knows he cannot own guns but openly possesses them anyway."

At a Nov. 30 sentencing hearing, Pratt handed down her decision. It wasn't what Cox or his supporters had hoped for. Two years in prison.

When he is released in 2020, Cox will be barred from commercial fishing for three years.

Stripped of his freedom and his old livelihood, the man who was Indiana’s caviar king now had a new identity: federal prisoner 03741-028.
