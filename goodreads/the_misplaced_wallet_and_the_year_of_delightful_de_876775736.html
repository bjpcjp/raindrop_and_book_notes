The Misplaced Wallet and The Year of Delightful Deception

The thirty zombies went silent and knelt in the darkness. They could hear the jangling of keys and police radio bleeps. Individuals fidgeted with their costumes and picked at their caked-on makeup. They tried to be quiet, hoping the police would leave. Had someone seen them enter? Nerves about the adventure they were undertaking mixed with the fear of being arrested. Breath was bated as they crouched, and waited. The police turned on their flashlights and started towards them.

It started in 2007, my junior year in college, when a fascination with abandoned spaces led my friend Nate and me to a lonely stretch of industrial Providence, where he left his wallet behind. The next day, we got an email from [email protected]:

Dearest Nate,

What happened? I miss you. You left me by the train tracks all alone, and I’m scared. The new people are treating me well, but I long to be back in your arms.

Love,

Your Wallet

Nate was relieved that his wallet was found, but we were more titillated by the creativity of the finding party. We felt it unjust to simply reply, “Thanks!” and collect the wallet. So, both to show appreciation and as an act of one-upmanship, we wrote and recorded a love song for the missing billfold. The first verse went:

You brush against my thighs as I walk down Thayer street,
You’re always there when I need to get something good to eat,
And though you’re filled with credit cards and crumpled-up receipts,
I know that what you’re really filled with is a love for me.

We attached the song, and sent it off to the Wallet. In response to the song, they created a Facebook profile for the Wallet. We tagged pictures of Nate’s thigh as the Wallet, and marked on Nate’s profile that he was in a relationship with the Wallet. The Wallet wrote back and promised, “to reunite soon.” A couple emails later, we still had no idea who had found the wallet, or when Nate was going to get it back.

The next day, we received a phone call: “Wilson, 501, get there before the doors close,” said a soft voice.

We sprinted up the stairs of Wilson Hall, racing to the roof. There is no “501,” or even a fifth floor to Wilson Hall. The fifth floor is the roof. As we careened around the last turn, a hippy-ish couple exited, smelling like weed.

“Hey, is there a wallet on the roof?” Nate asked.

“No man, but there’s, like, a rose…”

We opened the door to the tarred roof as the sun set. In a corner, we discovered an empty wine bottle holding a red rose. Next to the bottle was Nate’s wallet. We opened it up; it was nearly empty. The only item inside was a love note in green marker cursive, the beginning of a treasure hunt.

We stood on the roof holding Nate’s empty wallet, dumbfounded. Nate needed his credit cards, his dorm keycard; he needed his Brown ID in order to sit for his exams in a week.

Walking slowly back down the stairs, we called back the number, and heard in a sensual voice: boop bop beep—This number does not exist…

Over the next few days, we went from building to building, following one clue to the next. We were led to classrooms and labs, searching for little green notes. Finally, one of the clues contained the call number of a book. We found this book in the economics section of the library. Inside was a note: You’re so close, look around, my heart is on my sleeve. Two shelves up, there was a book with a pink heart sticker on its sleeve. We opened it up and discovered it had been hollowed out to create a compartment, inside of which were all of Nate’s credit cards, IDs, and receipts.

Standing in the stillness of the library aisle, we felt an odd mixture of relief and sadness. Nate was certainly comforted to have his items back, and the knowledge that we had unraveled this hunt was satisfying. However, we still had no idea who these people were. Further still, the spark, this adventure, appeared to be over. The feeling that we were a part of something vibrant and alive was fading.

“So, that’s it?” I asked.

“I guess so,” said Nate. “Although I still want to find them. People like this, we just have to know, right?”

The thought of ending it there was disheartening. All week we had an air of excitement about us that we hadn’t felt since we were young kids. This wasn’t about getting the wallet back, or even meeting these people. It was about embracing an idea of ourselves as people who valued spontaneity and delight in our lives.

“We should reward them.” I said.

“Wine on a rooftop?”

“And a treasure hunt for them to find it?”

“A more difficult one for sure…”

Nate was on the roof of Faunce Hall holding a bottle of wine; I was back at the dorm. Seven p.m. was the meet time, but it came and went. 7:10, then 7:15, and Nate was getting worried. The clues we made had disappeared the previous day. Had someone else taken them? The sun was setting again over College Hill. Finally, the door to the roof opened, and out walked a man in a tuxedo with coattails and a top hat. He carried a magic wand. He stepped up to Nate.

“Are you the person who found my wallet?” Nate asked. The man glared at Nate, sizing him up, then replied with the speed and tone of a Bond villain.

“I am not. But I know of them. You may call me: The Magician. One of them would like to speak with you.”

The Magician pulled out a walkie-talkie and began speaking into it in fluent French. “Oui, Oui, Il est ici. Oui, Il a la vino”—Nate took Latin in school—“Here she is.”

The woman on the walkie-talkie rambled. She thanked him for the wine and the treasure hunt and said what fond memories she had of such and such and so and so…

Nate’s phone rang, another unknown number, a man’s voice on the other end.

“Hey is this Nate? Oh good, I’m so sorry, I’m one of the guys who found your wallet. There’s a couple of us, we’re on our way to meet you, we’re running late, I just didn’t want you to think we weren’t coming.”

Now, Nate was really confused. “It’s totally fine, don’t worry about it, The Magician is already here.”

“Who’s already there?”

“The Magician. You know, The Magician.”

“Nate, I don’t know what you’re talking about.”

Nate looked up at the grinning Magician. It all happened so fast. His magic wand was a sparkler, which he lit and threw at Nate. The Magician pulled out a packet and threw it on the ground, sending plumes of purple smoke whirling around them. He grabbed the bottle of wine, and ran off the roof.

Nate called me on the phone, screaming in joy. “You are NOT going to believe this.” Again, a mixture of emotions washed over us: we were disappointed that we still didn’t know who these people were, but excited that they shared our spirit. We should have known they would respond to our treasure hunt with yet more madness. It confirmed our desire to come back, to discover their identities and one-up these people in a way that would be near impossible to top. For the next year, we planned.

The abandoned South Providence Rail Tunnel runs for 1.5 miles underneath College Hill. It’s one track and was partially flooded at the time, strewn with rusted shopping carts and oil drums. Graffiti spilled from the entrance and faded as the tunnel extended. Little soda-straw stalactites hung from the ceiling. At the far end was an overturned car, along with two broken drum sets and a smashed TV, remnants from some illicit party. There were more frightening things too, like a bird skeleton nailed to a plaque on the wall. Inside, sound traveled so well that you could hold a conversation from one end of the tunnel to the other. It is the darkest and quietest place I have ever been, and when we stopped moving and stood completely still, we could hear our own heartbeats.

The tunnel spills out into an empty lot visible from the street. As fortune would have it, a nearby construction project had placed a dumpster right in front of the door, shielding it from view. Using this advantage, we hopped a fence, ran down the short hill to the entrance, and over the course of a week, slowly cut through the welding keeping the door shut. Once it had been cut, we soldered our own latch and lock to the door so that no one else could enter. With this space in mind, we devised a plan.

I would dress up as a wizard and meet the three of them on the quad at midnight. We still had not met them, but through some clever Facebook sleuthing we’d learned their identities. I’d lead them down to the tunnel, giving them false instructions for a quest. We’d get in the tunnel, and shut the door behind us. I’d begin giving them the first challenge, at which point the door would open up and in would come two of our friends dressed as police officers. “Freeze, Providence Police!” they’d say, with convincing New England accents. They’d put us in handcuffs. I’d drop character, and begin pleading with the fake police.

Then, from around the corner, we’d hear the beating of a huge drum and see the flicker of light. Fear would enter into their hearts. Around the corner would come an army of zombies, plodding their way through the ankle-high water, carrying torches and beating the drum, craving brains. The fake police would be banging on the suddenly locked door. The zombies would arrive with terrifying sluggishness and ‘eat the brains’ of the cops. They’d grab me and The Three by the arms and haul us to the middle of the tunnel, where Nate would be dressed up in a king’s costume and sitting upon a throne.

“Master, may we eat these trespassers?” one zombie would say.

“No. Not these three,” Nate would say. “These three did a good thing for me a long time ago.” And then, Nate would whip out his guitar and sing the song he wrote for The Wallet, with new verses added for each part of the adventure. At the end he’d banish them from the tunnel, warning them to never return, and bid the zombies to devour me instead. The three would leave the tunnel to my pained wizard screams…

Building the throne room took hours. In the tunnel, your fear leaves you and you start to feel at home in the darkness and silence. We dragged large rubbish into a big pile in the center to make the throne room. We took old croquet mallets, stuck them upside down in the dirt, and put masks on top to mimic severed heads. We brought a high-backed chair and taped three tiki torches to the back to create a throne. We laid out towels as a red carpet, and lined the carpet with floor candles. The candles followed the carpet and opened out into a circle. Lighting them all, and extinguishing our flashlights, we were quieted by the mood of the scene we had constructed.

Outside the tunnel, Nate acquired wizard, police and king costumes. We found a guy who did zombie makeup. We sent message to The Walleteers to meet us on the quad one evening at midnight.

Some of our friends, including my little sister and my mother, came down to Providence. Thirty zombies in total, we met outside the art building, briefed everyone, and began leading people into the tunnel in groups of five or six. Once everyone was inside, we closed the door. Nate and I had been in the tunnel before, but most had not. Each new group took in the space with awe and anxiety. “We’re going to be murdered,” my mother said, smiling.

Everyone tied trash bags to their feet and walked to the throne room. The makeup guy hopped around, splashing people with paint, helping them rip shirts, and teaching proper zombie walks. We lit the floor candles and tiki torches.

I left to go change into my wizard gear. Elation washed over me as I strolled down the street, hardly believing any of this was happening. And then I saw cops. Real cops.

I turned around and began to run towards the tunnel, hoping to head them off. Will they think we’re a cult? Will my family and friends get arrested? I shouted, “Hey!” and waved my arms. The police ignored my yells and they hopped the fence. I got to the edge and saw that there were even more police at the bottom of the hill by the entrance, five or six total, one directing traffic, leading my friends out of the tunnel, a sad stream of zombies walking with their heads hung low.

Nate and I found each other on the street. There was an exasperation to our talk. We always knew this was a possibility, but it was easier to live in the fantasy where everything went right, where it was possible to pull something like this off. In the world as it actually happens, not everything works out.

I met The Walleteers on the quad at midnight, dressed as the wizard. They introduced themselves by their nicknames: The French Connection, The Puppet Master, and The Magician, who threw flame from his hand as he spoke. I led them away from the quad and gave them their false instructions, but before long I broke character and removed my wig.

I told them of all the work we’d done: the tunnel we’d broken into, the police, the scene that had almost taken place this evening. I told them we had something remarkable planned for them and it just wasn’t going to work out. But, leading them back into the quad, I asked, “Would you mind a quick consolation experience instead?”

In our absence from the quad, Nate and friend set up a new throne room on the steps of Brown’s most majestic building. They’d arranged and lit the floor candles in a cascade down the steps, and the zombies were lined up with outstretched jazz hands. I knelt before King Nate in his throne and The Walleteers followed suit. Nate deigned from his throne and approached.

“I couldn’t bear to see us graduate without having the chance to properly meet. Things haven’t gone to plan tonight, but so it goes. Still, I’ve prepared something for you all, with the help of my friends. I hope you enjoy it.”

Nate pulled out his guitar and started to sing. The zombies sang along, and the fake police had bongos and a woodblock, and random people stopped to watch. The song built and built and finished. Everyone cheered and clapped, and the zombies began hugging and congratulating each other and talking animatedly of the evening. Nate walked the final few steps towards The Walleteers and dropped his character.

“Hi, I’m Nate. It is so wonderful to meet you.”
