Why do so many football coaches emphasize Southern accents? Linguistics experts explain

On Dec. 1, 2021, Brian Kelly was introduced as the 34th head coach in LSU football history. During the hour-long news conference, he sounded like Brian Kelly, the Boston-bred football coach who established himself as one of the sport’s premier coaches during the previous dozen years at Notre Dame.

Later that night, he attended an LSU men’s basketball game. He walked onto the hardwood to officially acquaint himself with the Tiger fan base and spoke 13 words into the mic that soon went viral.

“It’s a great night to be a Tiger! I’m here with my family …” and at that point, it didn’t matter what Kelly said afterward. The pronunciation of just one word — “family”—  served as an immediate giveaway that Kelly was trying to present himself as a Tiger, as a Louisianan, as someone just like the fans there to welcome him to town. (To add further fascination to the topic, the writer of this piece and a linguistic professor both heard Kelly say, “It’s a great day to be a Tiger!” Several colleagues heard different, swearing Kelly instead said, “It’s a great night to be a Tiger!” Remember the optical illusion of whether you saw a gold dress or a blue dress? Here’s an accent version of it.)

College football is tethered to the South unlike any other region in the country. Language is often our first impression socially. It’s human nature to want to belong. So if you’re a newcomer to the South trying to adapt or a lifer holding on to your roots despite leaving long ago, the connective tissue is often what first rolls off the tongue.

But what is at the root of such a psychological desire? The reason is both simple and complex: Sports have influenced language, said Dr. Andrew Bray, professor of linguistics at Carleton College.

“A Brian Kelly-type might not necessarily be trying to sound Southern,” Bray said. “But he’s trying to sound football-oriented because of the historical importance of football to the South and the historical representation of Southern states and the football population.”

For coaches determined to convince players to join their teams, that means dipping into a certain dialect — well-delivered or not — to project expertise or familiarity. They may puff up a persona to connect with an audience from a specific region, such as the South, where many of college football’s top recruits reside. Some players might see it as a moment of vulnerability by unleashing the whole imitation-is-the-sincerest-form-of-flattery formula. Others might be immediately turned off by inauthenticity.

“There is also a solidarity dimension: How does that accent make you feel? Do you feel that person is relatable?” said Dr. Peggy Renwick, professor of linguistics at Georgia. “If a person doesn’t have a strong regional accent, you don’t have anything intrinsic to pick up on.”

What’s interesting is that almost everyone does it: We all use language to signal something to an audience or as a way to connect with others. Research even suggests there is value in it. And so while Kelly’s clip might have generated a lot of well-deserved laughs, it was rooted in something we can all relate to: trying to connect with others.

The four-word slogan of the Southeastern Conference that has ruled the landscape of the sport for decades now is known to every fervent follower of the sport:

It just means more. 

The slogan travels well beyond the bright lights of some of the biggest stadiums around. It reflects a specific slice of culture in the U.S. that takes immense pride in the region’s individual and collective history with the sport. Since 2000, 19 national championships have been claimed by teams most of America would include in the South.

Dr. Doug Thompson, professor of history at Mercer University, teaches the history of the American South. On his first or second day of class every semester, he’ll ask his new students to outline which states they perceive as being part of the region. They list off the usual ones: Louisiana, Alabama, Mississippi, Georgia, Florida.

But when he asks if Delaware, Maryland, West Virginia and Virginia are in consideration, they often say no. Those four states are south of the Mason-Dixon line. If he asks about Kentucky, which is also south of the line, they’ll often say yes.

“Then I say, ‘So the SEC,’” Thompson said. “I would call it a co-evolution of thinking about the South and football. It’s how we attach memory to a place.”

Place is where speech patterns and varying forms of communication are born. With a culture as niche as college football, an accent or a familiar saying can go a long way in helping a leader ease nerves or relate to another person’s experience.

As with the stereotype that most Americans think the typical British accent aligns with a more sophisticated and educated persona, you can make a similar argument that someone with any sort of Southern twang might sound more knowledgeable in football Xs and Os as opposed to someone from Southern California or the Bronx.

Cincinnati head coach Scott Satterfield grew up in rural northern North Carolina, where accents are so strong that he joked recently that members of his own family are still hard to understand. Satterfield said the concept of language as an icebreaker will never be lost on coaches who have ties to the South.

“When I’m recruiting somebody from the South, certainly it’s no problem — I can talk like I always talk,” he said. “But when I talk to someone from the North or the Midwest, I’m a little more cognizant of what I’m saying. You just don’t want to say a phrase that maybe they would not have heard of.”

With the regionality of college football recruitment being basically eliminated in the social media era, a coach like USC’s Lincoln Riley, born and raised in West Texas, might play up his regional tone if he’s chasing a recruit in his home state.

“When you leave the region, the one thing you try to do is over-perform the Southernness so you can remind people that I am Southern, football is Southern, most of my players get to the NFL,” Bray said.

Oregon’s Dan Lanning, for example, grew up in Missouri, but has spent the bulk of his coaching career in the South. The Ducks have routinely had recruiting classes ranked among the highest in the sport since Lanning was hired in December 2021 and signed high-profile recruits from Alabama, Texas and Florida. Studying Lanning’s news conference tape, he can adopt any number of slight accents, from casual Midwestern to more typical Southern, rolling his R’s on “experience” or accentuating the “a” in “awesome day to be a Duck, man,” to changing the overall pronunciation of “program” to “progrum.”

“Sociolinguistics would be happy to hypothesize that the football persona has a Southern tinge to it,” said Renwick. “Having aspects of that in your speech could be to your advantage if you want to project that as part of your persona.”

Such communicative minutiae can pay off, too.

Dr. Patricia Bestelmeyer is a senior lecturer in psychology at Bangor University in Wales with a focus on paralinguistics. In a 2024 study, she found that people tended to trust people who sounded similar to them.

“We activate regions in the brain that are usually related to positive affect when we listen to speakers who sound like us compared to other accents,” Bestelmeyer wrote in an email.

Now, nobody is committing to Georgia because of Kirby Smart’s Georgia drawl or to Clemson because of Dabo Swinney’s often theatrical Alabama-raised inflections. But being relatable right off the bat with something as simple as language can go a long way to making an interaction — like, say, a recruiting pitch — more successful.

“It is a benefit to them, relating to people who belong to the football subculture,” Renwick said. “It could be fans, could be families of players, it could be a persona that includes regional aspects and also aspects of masculinity that are relevant, too.”

In his infamous LSU intro, Kelly was burned by many people for the way he pronounced “family” because he fixated on the a-vowel when the easier Southern target vowel would’ve been the I-vowel. Bray studied Kelly’s intro presser and found that it took him 0.4 seconds to pronounce “family” in the suit and tie. That doubled to 0.8 seconds with the pronounced Southern accent at the basketball game.

Both Renwick and Bray studied various clips of college football coaches, from Kelly to Riley to Minnesota coach P.J. Fleck, and noticed that so many of them come off as bidialectal — meaning they can turn on or off varying dialects depending on who they’re talking to or the nature of the topic they’re discussing.

Bray mentioned former Alabama head coach Nick Saban was one of the most notable examples. As someone who grew up in West Virginia, Saban has spent the majority of his life coaching in the South. During his time leading the Crimson Tide, Bray said Saban routinely dialed up the Southern parts of his speech because that’s part of the job. But since retiring in 2024 and having a role on ESPN’s College GameDay, Saban more often uses a mainstream American English accent.

In total, scholars and linguistic experts say there are at least eight identified dialects of the South that range from Texas to the Appalachians. Thompson said the perception of what sounds Southern to folks outside the South is what also perpetuates the reality that the football culture there is stronger.

“They have similar identities, but the South has this thing where it’s a region unto itself,” Thompson said. “For me, that’s what fandom is: I’m part of a larger group. I don’t like them in any other context, but on Saturday afternoon or evening, they’re my friends.”

Back coaching his alma mater West Virginia for a second time, Rich Rodriguez deadpanned when asked about his corner of the regional cadre: “I’m a hillbilly.”

He’s happy to be back home, where he said fans of the Mountaineers “cry when we win and cry when we lose.” And when it’s time for West Virginia to host rival Pittsburgh in the bitter Backyard Brawl on Sept. 13, “there will be 60,000 people in the stands, and 15,000 in the parking lot drinking moonshine, wishing they could get in, but not really remembering why they came.”

The son of a coal miner, Rodriguez said that his thick Appalachian accent felt most out of place during his much-maligned time coaching the dormant powerhouse Michigan. That was back when he worried about such things. Now, he says, he doesn’t need to cater to anyone.

“My give-a-s— meter left a long time ago,” he said. “You gotta be who you are.”
