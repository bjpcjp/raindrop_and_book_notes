Why I wouldn't invest in open-source companies, even though I ran one.

Tags: intellectual-property, open-source, platforms, prodmgmt

Bookmark ID: 876778386

After more than 12.000 Github stars, two successful open-source projects, a failed open-core company, and a successful prop-tech one*, I feel more than ever that giving your product away for free is just as bad a business strategy as it sounds.

Yet VCs seem to love open-source businesses - and it's easy to see why: Given the (pre-IPO) success of the Uber and WeWork playbook of venture subsidized, money-losing offerings that quickly take over markets and squash competition, offering your product for free seems a logical step.

But for "Free Open Source Software" (FOSS), this is a fallacy. Where WeWork's opponents had to work profitably within market conditions, the world of FOSS is full of strong and established offerings with a zero-dollar price tag.

FOSS as a distribution channel

Ok - but what about FOSS as a distribution channel? Open-Source indeed makes it easier to penetrate large enterprises. Especially for highly technical solutions, outbound sales is incredibly hard. If you're selling project management software, your salespeople can ring up just about anybody. Yet, for your novel database, batch processing solution or decentralized message queue, the potential customers are buried deep within the guts of an organization.

But even inbound lead generation via open-source isn't as straight forward as it sounds. OS is usually distributed via package managers, either on a source code level (NPM, crate, Maven) or a system-level (apt, rpm, yum). These provide very little insight, if any, about download stats and distribution and no insight at all about the actual organizations that downloaded your solution 500 times last week alone. Instead, you either have to hide your product behind a conversion-killing sign-up page, or you are stuck waiting for inbound leads.

Once they arrive, it is usually a senior developer from the customer side getting in touch. This is a two-edged sword. Techies hold increasing sway within their organizations. If they are satisfied, they might become champions of your solution and will fight your cause internally. But fight they must. Only a handful of engineering-centric companies give their developers any say in budget allocation. For most companies, your tech-champion will have to forward you to the decision-makers and purchasing departments - of which they tend to be blissfully unaware.

Community

So what about community? Doesn't open-source mean that others will develop your product? Well, sort of - if you have a truly free open-source software product, people will chime in with contributions. This is especially valuable if your product thrives on a rich ecosystem of plugins and integrations (think WordPress). But the vast bulk of development will always rest on your core team. The majority of contributions also tend to be of lower quality - unsurprising, given the knowledge asymmetry between your core team and the devs contributing in their spare time. 

But that's not the full picture. Employers are increasingly using Github metrics, especially open-source contributions, to make recruitment decisions - and developers have learned to game this system. So you'll see countless contributions of the "I changed your readme headline to say 'great product'" - variety, which will take time to review and reject.

Still, getting free contributions sounds pretty neat, right? Well, not really. If you are running a commercial business on top of your open-source solution, it is paramount that you own the product's full IP. That means that you will have to ask every contributor to sign over the rights to their work to you - which will make them rightfully feel utilized and which will quickly stifle their motivation.

Even worse. Your community might be an unpaid PR and Evangelism squad. But as soon as you make a commercial decision that benefits your company, e.g., change your license or release a certain feature as part of your enterprise, rather than open-core version, the same people will turn into your most vocal critics, and Google results will be dominated by angry Tweets and Reddit posts for weeks to come.

OS Business Models & Conflicts of Interest

Where FOSS really puts a strain on commercial business though, is in its inherent conflicts of interest. Open-Source is not a business model; it's a distribution channel - the real business model tends to be one or a combination of the following:

  • Open-Core: The company open-sources a slimmed-down version of its product and sells a fully-featured "enterprise version" on top (e.g., Kafka/Confluent or Docker/Docker EE)
  • Hosted Version: The company offers a fully managed hosted version of its product ( e.g., MongoDB or Grafana)
  • Support & Consultancy:  The company offers support and consultancy services around its open-source product (e.g., Elastic or MongoDB)

Each of these models come with inherent conflicts of interest. Open-Core businesses will have to choose for every feature if it goes into open-source or enterprise. Sometimes this decision is clear cut, e.g., when it comes to enterprise-specific functionality such as Active Directory integration, audit trails, or permission management. Other times, "enterprise" simply means "large scale" - and the decision to withhold clustering, monitoring support, or failover will make the open-source version deliberately worse.

Companies that offer hosted versions of their product have an easier life. But here too, making the product easier to deploy, monitor, and scale can directly cannibalize the hosting profits.

Even companies that offer consultancy and support around a truly free OS offering will have to decide how detailed their documentation should be, how vast their catalog of integration examples, and how helpful their tutorials.

Licensing & Vulnerability

Similarly, licensing has to navigate the tension between fostering community growth and retaining ownership. "True" FOSS licenses such as MIT or Apache 2 give substantial ownership to the public. This might motivate contributors, but makes for a poor choice for Open-Core businesses. 

Copyleft licenses such as AGPL force adopters to publish changes back to the source repo or offer them via network protocols. This is meant to deter third parties from offering competing hosted services, but is legally murky and can prompt a customer's internal legal departments to prevent adoption altogether.

Changing a license during the lifetime of a project is certainly possible, but usually met with a violent community backlash. Even worse, as licenses can't be changed after the fact, earlier versions with more permissive licenses can present a substantial attack vector for companies moving to open-core as e.g., Elastic had to learn when AWS published a competing OS version of Elastic Search.

Exit Valuations

There have been substantial exits of open-source companies, e.g. RedHat (acquired for 32 billion by IBM) or Magento (acquired for 1.7 billion by Adobe). Still, by far and large, OS is more of a detriment when it comes to exit valuations. Especially strategic buyers interested in the technology rather than community or ecosystem will put a heavy discount on the value of technology that's available for free.

Conclusion

There are numerous examples of highly successful open-source companies: RedHat, HashiCorp, MongoDB, Elastic, to name just a few. And it would be too simple to say that these succeeded despite, not because of open-source.

Instead, it is important to understand the exact mechanism of open-source that led to their success and to the failure of so many others. Open-Source acts like the rubber bands on a funfair catapult. The same forces that initially pull the capsule up quickly (low friction adoption, community support) will ultimately pull it back down (community hostile to monetization, IP and licensing issues, etc.). To succeed, a company has to reach escape velocity and get the timing just right. But even then, large scale open-source adoption does not necessarily translate into commercial success (see Docker, Inc.), and companies will remain vulnerable to attacks (see Elastic).

The question then is: Is open-source a better business strategy than a more conventional, proprietary tech model? And the answer - at least to me - is a resounding "No". The ratio of failed OS businesses to successful ones is worse than in prop-tech; revenue kicks in much later, business model pivots are hampered by community resistance, and licensing issues leave OS businesses vulnerable throughout their lifetime. Instead, why not do what traditional businesses are doing, sell a product, and simply charge for the value it provides?
