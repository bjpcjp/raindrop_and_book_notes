The Patriots’ dynasty began back in 2002, when Bill Belichick came up with a masterful defensive game plan to stifle what looked like an unstoppable Rams offense known as the “greatest show on turf.” Seventeen years later, in Super Bowl LIII, he accomplished an eerily similar feat — devising another masterful defensive game plan to stop another explosive Rams offense, one that ranked second in total yards in the regular season. The NFL’s coaching prodigy Sean McVay was taught a harsh lesson by the old master that dons the hoodie with cut-off sleeves.

After the game, Belichick said, “we felt like we had to put something together to neutralize the running game and their big-play play action passes on early downs. That’s where they killed people all year.”

To limit those explosive plays, the Patriots completely switched up their coverage philosophy. After playing predominately man coverage all year, they played exclusively soft zone coverages on early downs with the intent of not getting beat deep.

“(The Patriots defense) mixed it up,” McVay said. “Really in the early downs, all they ended up was playing some single high buzz structures and some quarters principles. Third down, they had their designers and things like that. They did a great job. It was a great game plan. There is no other way to say it, but I got outcoached.”

One of the keys to the Rams’ offense is its simplicity. They don’t run a ton of concepts or formations but all of their plays work in a system together and everything from their run game to their play-action game to their screen game was indistinguishable from the pre-snap to the first few seconds of the play. Aside from the Bears game in Week 14, it seemed like every time an opponent came up with a solution for the Rams’ offense, McVay eventually had a response. Belichick countered with his own “simple” game plan: a 6-1 front that had answers for all of the Rams’ bread-and-butter plays.

6-1 Front

The Patriots played some 6-2 fronts throughout the year, so the 6-1 shouldn’t have taken the Rams completely by surprise. I actually correctly predicted that they would get into an adjusted front with six men on the line with a safety playing an edge in my Super Bowl Preview. The Patriots’ defensive line was just too susceptible to the run whenever they were in a four-man line. Getting six players on the line of scrimmage didn’t allow the Rams’ offensive line to double-team and climb to the second level on their lethal outside zone play.

On the strong side, the Patriots had a defensive tackle in a 2-technique (head up on the guard), the end lined up inside of the tight end, while safety Patrick Chung played the edge. Chung’s ability to hold his ground against the run and cover was key to making this front work. After he was hurt in the second half, versatile inside linebacker Dont’a Hightower moved to that position but the Rams didn’t attempt to expose him in coverage.

On the weak side, the defense single-gapped with a defensive tackle in the A-gap, Hightower in the B-gap, and an end in the C-gap. Linebacker Kyle Van Noy played the edge on the weak side.

The principles of this 6-1 are as follows:

  • Both defensive ends played a 2-gap technique and read the blocks of the offensive tackle to their side. The ends could have played the inside or outside gap depending on their reads. This strategy muddied up the read for the running back on outside zone.
  • Van Noy, who played the edge, had to dominate the weak side to stop weak outside zone.
  • Edge players (Chung, Van Noy) were responsible for the flats in coverage.
  • The wide alignment of both edge players discouraged two of the Rams base plays: fly sweep and boot
  • There is no inside linebacker to the strong side but since all the gaps are clogged, the idea is that the front will slow down the running back enough for the secondary to come up and support the run.

Secondary Run Support

McVay said the Patriots played a lot of quarters principles, or cover 4. Against the Rams, the Patriots secondary played back and only came up on the run when they were sure that the ball was handed off to the running back. This is actually a similar strategy to what the Lions, Bears, and Eagles had some success with in the regular season. With two weeks to game plan, Belichick built on that strategy and nearly perfected it. Again, the idea is that the secondary didn’t have to come up hard in run support because the jammed gaps would slow down the running back enough to give them time to slow-play the run. Otherwise, they are focused on stopping explosive play-actions.

Hightower also could slow-play the run from the weak side and didn’t have to fast flow to the front side for the same reasons that the secondary could slow-play it. Even if he did read the run a beat slow and blockers were able to get to him, he’s physical enough to destroy the blocks like he did on this play:

The ends were able to put themselves in great position to defend the Rams’ core plays by reading the blocks of the offensive tackles to their side. If the tackles engaged with them, the ends would play head up and hold their ground like they did in the first clip in the article. By making their reads correctly, they could also stop the fly sweep and boot.

Here, Deatrich Wise Jr. (No. 91) read the block of right tackle Rob Havenstein (No. 79) perfectly. Havenstein took a reach step but didn’t engage with Wise Jr. and kept working to the perimeter. Wise Jr. widened out with him on his read step but when he realized Havenstein wasn’t engaging with him, he got his head inside to look for the fly sweep. His job was to string the play out for Chung, who was responsible for containing the play. Chung did an awesome job of holding his ground against tight end Tyler Higbee and stopping the play in combination with Wise Jr..

By having only the end and edge player primarily responsible for the fly sweep, linebackers and members of the secondary didn’t have to react to the fly motion. Being able to stop the fly sweep for a minimal gain early in the game was important because the Rams were able to constantly get previous opponents out of position with fly motion and fakes.

On this play, Havenstein stepped away from Wise Jr., so he shuffled with him while keeping his shoulders squared to the line of scrimmage. If he rushed upfield and chased the play-fake he would have made himself an easy target for receiver Robert Woods to block and Goff would have gotten to the edge unimpeded with ample time to throw like he did on numerous occasions in the regular season and the postseason with this same boot concept. Wise Jr. was in a good position to fend off Woods’ block and chased Goff down.

The secondary did a beautiful job of covering every receiver on the boot play behind Wise Jr.. The second Patriots zone coverage that McVay mentioned was single-high buzz or cover 3- buzz, which is a cover 3 zone with the strong safety dropping down to play the hook zone in the short to intermediate middle of the field. The safety buzz allows the secondary to disguise the coverage by giving quarterback Jared Goff a 2-deep pre-snap look.

Again, the edge players were responsible for the flats in the 6-1 front. Van Noy saw receiver Josh Reynolds running in the flats from across the formation and trucked him to the ground — that’s one way of covering the flats — and Goff had nowhere to go with the ball.

Cover 4

The Patriots played a lot of soft cover 4 rather than an aggressive pattern-match cover 4 because they didn’t want to get beat deep. In their zone coverages, New England’s defensive backs gingerly dropped back and kept their eyes on Goff with a soft focus on receivers. They kept everything in front of them and rallied up to make tackles on short throws. They wanted to force the Rams to go the length of the field on lengthy drives.

However, the Rams still had an opportunity to hit a big play against the Patriots’ cover 4 early in the game but Goff didn’t see Brandin Cooks get behind the secondary. With 4:19 remaining the fourth quarter, the Rams called their Mills concept with Cooks running a deep post and Woods running a dig route.

Goff’s first read should be Cooks on the deep post but he might have decided pre-snap that the the secondary was playing too deep and thought the post wasn’t a viable option. Goff also seemed to think that Cooks’ deep route would take his corner and safety Devin McCourty, who was lined up on Cooks’ side, with him which would leave a void for Woods to run his crosser into.

Instead, McCourty read Goff’s eyes and jumped Woods, which left Cooks open deep. Goff locked on to Woods based on his pre-snap read and had to clutch the ball when he saw McCourty jump the route. Goff’s hesitation allowed the rush to get to him and he was forced to ground the ball for an incomplete pass.

The Rams coaches upstairs likely saw the missed opportunity in the first quarter and knew that they would go back to the play later in the game. In the third quarter, down by three points, the Rams drove to the 30 yard line and had a chance to take the lead with a touchdown and McVay went back to the Mills concept.

Goff knew he had to look off McCourty again to get him to jump the crosser. It worked but Goff seemed like he wanted to make sure that McCourty stepped up rather than anticipating that he would. He looked off McCourty for too long and threw to Cook late. Luckily for Devin McCourty, his brother, cornerback Jason McCourty, might have saw how open Cook was the last time that the Rams ran Mills in the first quarter and was in position to make a play. Jason could have easily lost focus and fell asleep on the backside but he kept dropping and made an incredible break on the ball.

2 play calls

According to Sports Illustrated’s Albert Breer, the Patriots defense got two play calls on every play. The Rams are great at communicating audibles quickly and snapping the ball before the defense could adjust. By having two plays called, the Patriots could quickly check into the second call without having to think about what to check into.

This Patriots’ two play call strategy took away the advantage of the Rams’ audible system. The favorable look that the Rams’ offense got when they made the audible wouldn’t be the same after the Patriots quickly checked out of it. It probably wasn’t easy to memorize two play calls every play for the Patriots’ own players but credit goes to their staff for simplifying their system enough to do it and to the players for being able to execute it on the game’s biggest stage.

Designers

When McVay said that the Patriots used their “designers” on third downs, he meant the Patriots’ exotically designed pressure schemes that former Patriots defensive coordinator and current head coach of the Miami Dolphins Brian Flores called on third downs or passing situations.

“It’s the one hundred twenty something explosive plays that they’ve had. We felt if we could make them drive it, make them earn it… and just make them run a lot of plays and get them in third down,” said Belichick. “We felt like we had a chance to get them off the field on third down.”

He was confident that the Rams protection scheme would have trouble blocking the Patriots designers. The Patriots had the highest pressure rate of any defense in the postseason and they did so primarily with scheme and teamwork rather than individual efforts. Their most frequently used designer was a nose tackle twist.

Here, the Patriots lined up a nose tackle Adam Butler (No. 70) over the center which caused the Rams to get into their man pass protection scheme, which meant that every offensive lineman was assigned a defender, rather then using a slide protection scheme which has zone principles.

In man protection, linemen can still pass off stunts but the Rams did a terrible job of doing that for the entirety of the game. They seemed focused on sticking with their assigned defender which left them susceptible to getting picked off like in the clip above. Butler attacked upfield on center John Sullivan’s outside shoulder while Van Noy got guard Roger Saffold to retreat by initially rushing right at him. After Saffold got deep enough to be even with Sullivan, Van Noy cut inside which caused Saffold to collide with Sullivan as Safford attempted to stay in front of Van Noy. Sullivan didn’t switch to Van Noy and he got a free lane right in Goff’s grill.

The Patriots sacked Goff four times and pressured him on 50% of drop back passes (including play-action straight drop backs not boot plays) primarily with various nose tackle twists. Former NFL offensive lineman Geoff Schwartz explains the techniques involved in these schemes further in depth in the clip below:

On third down and passing situations, the Patriots went back to what they do best — play man coverage. Even when they got into their man coverage schemes, they made Goff hesitate and fooled him by disguising where help was coming from.

Here, the Patriots gave their basic bunch coverage defense look initially. It looked like every receiver would get a one-on-one but after the ball is snapped, safety Devin McCourty rotated down and bracketed Woods with his brother, Jason.

Though playing zone coverage was a great change-up, New England’s ability to play man would win them the game. With 4:29 remaining the game, down by 7,  the Rams drove the ball all the way to the Patriots’ 27 yard line. On first down, Goff threw a perfect pass to Cooks on a fade route in the end zone which he dropped because Gilmore sneakily grabbed one of Cooks’ arms before the ball arrived. Yes, being able to slyly get away with slight holds or interference is an actual skill for defensive backs.

On the next play, the Rams broke the huddle with little time remaining on the play clock. Flores waited for a critical moment to call his overload blitz and he picked the perfect time.

The Patriots played cover 0 (man-to-man with no deep help) and sent four rushers to the right side where the Rams only had three blockers. Because the play clock was expiring, Goff did not have time to change the protection and to snap the ball.

“Obviously, I should have thrown it away,” he told the NFL Network’s Michael Silver after the game . “I knew it was ‘zero’ — of course I did — but I thought I could make a play. I didn’t realize Gilmore was staring at me, and I threw too early. I put it in a bad spot. It was dumb. It was stupid. I will learn from it. But it really hurts right now.”

Even though there was a free rusher, Goff could have stepped into this throw but he rushed it and threw off his back foot. The ball sailed right into Gilmore’s waiting hands. In this coverage and situation, corners would typically play flat-footed to get ready to jump a short hot route. The Patriots seemed to have picked up on film the Rams’ “hot throw” against cover 0 was a fade because Gilmore opened up his hips and bailed back at the snap of the ball even. The biggest defensive play of the game was the culmination of meticulous study and preparation with a clever scheme and play-calling.

Lessons for the young Rams

The Rams went all-in on winning a Super Bowl with splashy trades and big signings and there might be some regression in their future but they will be back in contention with McVay at the helm. He was taken to school by the best defensive coach of all-time but this sort of game should be an awakening for him. His system is obviously effective but he needs to add more counters. For example, the Rams need a better quick game package from under-center. They were unable to punish the Patriots’ 6-1 front from their base formations with quick passes for solid gains to stay on schedule. I was surprised that McVay didn’t force the Patriots out of their 6-1 by shifting into empty formations, spreading the defense out, and taking advantage of the Patriots’ slow linebackers with Gurley, who I don’t believe is hurt, in the passing game.

For Goff, he has to get more consistent with his anticipation. There were openings in the Rams’ zone coverage but he couldn’t take advantage because he was late on throws. His receivers also have to do a better job of sitting in the voids in those zones. Yes, the Patriots surprised the Rams with their high zone usage and the Rams were likely practicing ways to beat man coverage for two weeks with very little time dedicated to beating zone but they should have been able to adapt.

In the end of the highly anticipated strategical matchup, McVay and his team couldn’t adapt to Belichick’s masterpiece of a game plan but McVay has proven his football acumen and he’ll learn from this experience. Instead of criticizing what should be the lowest moment in McVay’s coaching career, we should appreciate Belichick’s genius. McVay often refers to the “illusion of complexity” to describe his offense but Belichick undressed that illusion and exposed its simplicity with his own “simple” game plan. Though the game might not have been exciting as fans liked, the ingenuity of the hoodie was as rewarding to piece together as any breakdown that I have ever done.
