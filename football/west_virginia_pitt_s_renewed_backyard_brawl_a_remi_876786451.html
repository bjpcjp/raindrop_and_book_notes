West Virginia-Pitt’s renewed Backyard Brawl a reminder of toll realignment

ARLINGTON, Texas — West Virginia coach Neal Brown brought up the return of the Backyard Brawl before anyone could ask, just 123 words into his opening statement at Big 12 media days.

The Mountaineers open the 2022 season at Pitt, a Thursday night showcase opportunity. It’s a rivalry with 104 meetings, one that dates back to 1895, but hasn’t been played since 2011. The Backyard Brawl is finally back, and Wednesday’s media event happened to mark 50 days until the game is renewed. Brown, ecstatic to talk about it, said unprompted that he’d favor playing the game every year. The teams are scheduled to play each other in four-year series from 2022 to ’25 and from 2029 to ’32.

“Those games are for our fans,” Brown said of this season’s nonconference matchups with Pitt and Virginia Tech, two of the Mountaineers’ longest rivals. “It’s important.”

Rivalries are only “back” if they’ve gone away, and the return of the Backyard Brawl is a reminder of what has been lost in college football due to conference realignment over the years — a topic top of mind once again with USC and UCLA’s recently revealed departures for the Big Ten in 2024. West Virginia’s trip to Virginia Tech in Week 4 renews a rivalry that has been played just twice since 2005, including WVU’s win last year. Oklahoma–Nebraska, another historic rivalry, returned last year as well to great fanfare.

And while the return of the Pitt game was a major media day topic for West Virginia, the potential end of Bedlam with Oklahoma’s impending departure for the SEC was a topic just two podiums over from Brown, where Oklahoma State head coach Mike Gundy sat.

“Bedlam’s history,” Gundy said. “Bedlam is not gonna be Bedlam when they leave the conference.”

Oklahoma State quarterback Spencer Sanders offered earlier, “Don’t take it personal, but you went away. I’m not gonna sit here and beg for it. It was a fun game while it lasted.”

Oklahoma State coach Mike Gundy said he doesn’t see a future for the Cowboys’ rivalry with Oklahoma. (Jerome Miron / USA Today)

This is what gets lost in realignment. Not the mirage of parity or the imaginary good ol’ days that weren’t about money (it was always about money). It’s the tangible loss of not playing a school an hour away after playing for more than a century.

The return of the Backyard Brawl is especially big for West Virginia because the Mountaineers lost basically all of their historic rivals over the years through no fault of their own. Penn State joined the Big Ten in 1993. Virginia Tech left the Big East for the ACC in 2004, and Pitt did the same in 2013. The Mountaineers jumped to the Big 12 to stay in a power conference. It was a necessary move at the time, but a decade later, the school still doesn’t have any major in-conference rivals, in part due to geography. The addition of Cincinnati to the Big 12 next season could help.

“We’ve got to be careful in this day and age with what makes college football different than professional sports,” Brown said.

Though Brown would love to play the Pitt game every year, he does not want to play multiple nonconference rivalries on the road, as is the case this year. In April, the schools announced the four additional Pitt matchups for 2029 to ’32. Athletic director Shane Lyons wants to keep it going, but also throw in some of those other rivalries. A series with Penn State begins next year. With a nine-game conference schedule, there’s only so much room.

“For me scheduling out, if I could sprinkle in Penn State, Maryland, Virginia Tech, some other games, that would be beneficial for us from a nonconference standpoint,” Lyons said. “Our fans want to play Pitt every year, but at the same time, if we’re only going to do a series, I think it’s pretty cool to have a four-year series, take a break, fill in with some other people and then come back to it.”

It also brings up the question, is it still a rivalry if it hasn’t been played in 11 years? Many West Virginia players have scant memories of the game. Senior defensive lineman Dante Stills, a West Virginia native whose father and brother also played for the Mountaineers, couldn’t immediately recall any favorite Pitt rivalry memories.

“I don’t really remember, I was nine or 10,” he said. “I just know it’s a big rival, we don’t like them, we’re an hour away. This is a game for the ages, one a lot of people have been wanting to see.”

That urgency from fans and the people around campus has been evident.

“I’m not really familiar, but after they popped up on the schedule, I found out real quick what the rivalry is,” cornerback Charles Woods said.

Brown has brought in former players during the spring and summer to talk about the history of the rivalry. The head coach himself hasn’t experienced the game and has leaned on analyst Jeff Casteel, who was a WVU assistant from 2001 to ’16, to educate him.

“When we came back after the bowl game, it’s been a carrot for our players,” Brown said. “We have a countdown clock we started going. For players, it’s given them a sense of urgency in their preparation.”

The last time the Backyard Brawl was played, West Virginia overcame a 10-point deficit to win and eventually play in the Orange Bowl. In 2007, a 4-7 Pitt team knocked the No. 2 Mountaineers out of the BCS national championship game. The 1975 game saw West Virginia beat Tony Dorsett the year before he won the Heisman. The 1965 matchup had a ridiculous 63-48 score.

These are the games and memories that get passed down by generations. It’s been a lonely decade for West Virginia fans, out on their own geographically from the rest of the Big 12. But the return of the Pitt rivalry this year and in the future is a reminder of the charm that seems to be dwindling from the sport. In 2020, Kansas and Missouri finally announced the return of their rivalry, beginning in 2025. Texas and Texas A&M, with their rivalry paused for more than a decade because of realignment, will be forced back together when the Longhorns join the Aggies in the SEC. As realignment potentially ramps up again, Wednesday highlighted the return of one rivalry and the potential loss of another, illustrating just how much this sport has been pulled in different directions.

“I want to do what’s best for college football,” Lyons said. “Hopefully this is trend-setting to say we’ve lost some of this in other leagues and other rivalries, and can they build those back? That was the pageantry of college football, the rivalry and your friends who went to school right down the road. That’s good for college football. That’s what we need to look at. Scheduling is more than just your conference. For your fans, what’s important to them?”
