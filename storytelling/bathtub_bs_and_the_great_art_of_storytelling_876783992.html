Bathtub BS and the Great Art of Storytelling

Tags: storytelling

Bookmark ID: 876783992

"On December 20 there flitted past us, absolutely without public notice, one of the most important profane anniversaries in American history, to wit, the seventy-fifth anniversary of the introduction of the bathtub into These States. Not a plumber fired a salute or hung out a flag. Not a governor proclaimed a day of prayer. Not a newspaper called attention to the day.”

So goes an article published in the New York Evening Mail on December 28, 1917, honoring the “forgotten” 75th anniversary of the modern bathtub. The author was H.L. Mencken, a renowned journalist and American English scholar, who wrote the article to test how easily Americans would believe what was written in print.

The story was entirely false, and riddled with absurdities. It went on to say that the original bathtub was not much more than a “glorified dishpan,” which required filling and emptying by a servant. When introduced in America, it sparked much public controversy as people believed bathing to be a health risk (!), until president Millard Fillmore popularized them by installing one in the White House in 1850.

Yet, despite these falsehoods, publications nationwide picked up the story, and Fillmore became known as the president who bucked convention, lifting the country to a new height of cleanliness.

Mencken published a confession eight years later in another well-known publication - The Chicago Tribune - yet, “facts” from the original article appeared in major publications for nearly 100 years. As recently as February 2004, The Washington Post ran a story naming Fillmore as the first president to install a bathtub in the White House. They issued a correction a few days later.

How could so many people, so many journalists, fall for this?

The biggest reason, in my opinion, is people love a good story. Mencken’s article had all the right components - it grabbed people’s attention and made them want to believe. Let’s examine:

1) A great intro (which I used above), that hinted at letting people in on something largely unknown.

2) Controversy. According to Mencken:

"On the one hand it was denounced as an epicurean and obnoxious toy from England, designed to corrupt the democratic simplicity of the Republic, and on the other hand it was attacked by the medical faculty as dangerous to health and a certain inviter of ‘phthisic, rheumatic fevers, inflammation of the lungs and the whole category of zymotic diseases.’"

Bathing dangerous to one’s health? Hard to imagine now, but even 75 years after the supposed invention, this was enough to pique people’s interest.

3) A hero. The fact that Millard Fillmore defied public opinion and gave the bathtub a chance made him a hero.

"The example of the President soon broke down all that remained of the old opposition, and by 1860, according to the newspaper advertisements of the time, every hotel in New York had a bathtub, and some had two and even three."

4) Third party validation. Mencken quoted "The Western Medical Repository" and the "Christian Register." These journals didn’t exist, but they sounded plausible. Citing these with specific dates lent credibility to the story, so people assumed he was presenting facts.

5) Great timing. Receptivity can vary greatly depending on what else is going on at the time. In this instance, people likely wanted a reprieve from wartime talk. When you’re telling or pitching a story today, you have to know what else is happening in the world of the reporters or audiences you’re trying to reach. If there’s another big story taking place, chances are they’re too busy to get to yours, even if they want to.

A famous quip is credited to Lord Arthur Balfour, prime minster of England in the early 1900s: “History does not repeat itself. Historians repeat each other.” This is why so much importance is put on the “exclusive” in PR - the first story to run. It sets the tone for ensuing coverage. Long after a story publishes - whether true or not - people will continue to share or cite it (so make sure the first one is accurate!).

Think we’re immune to this kind of thing today? Not hardly. In the couple of weeks I spent researching this post, I came across several complete falsehoods being spread by “social media gurus” in the interest of establishing thought leadership for themselves. These weren't necessarily being spread with ill intentions, but it just goes to show that when all it takes is a click to share a quick fact with hundreds or thousands of people, it’s all too easy for bad information to proliferate.

It is easier today, however, for people to check the facts and figure out whether you're being honest, or know what you’re talking about. If not, you will be outed sooner or later. When you have, chances are it will be too late - you’ll have lost your audience’s trust and it won’t be easy to win back. Funny enough, although Mencken later admitted the bathtub story was a hoax, so many people had long since believed it that no one wanted to think they’d been duped, and it was his confession that was often regarded as false.

If you’re an entrepreneur, a PR or marketing practitioner, don’t underestimate the value of a good story. Take the time to ensure yours (or your clients’) is great. As Mencken’s and all of the other great stories we share each day demonstrate, we’re all waiting to be captivated – just try us!
