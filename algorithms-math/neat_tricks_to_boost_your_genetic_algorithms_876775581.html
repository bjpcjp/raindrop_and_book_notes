Genetic Algorithm (or Evolutionary Algorithm) — A simple, yet very powerful optimization technique which has seen resurgence in popularity recently. From evolving reinforcement learning model, neural architecture search, or plain-old feature selection, Genetic Algorithm (hereafter abbreviated as GA) offers a strong contender even to the most sophisticated Deep Learning — even big names, such as Uber and Google, are taking a shot at GA.

If you are new to GA, you might find this article useful.

However powerful GA might be, it is not without its shortcomings. The thing is, GA is very simple to implement, yet extremely hard to master. New data scientists trying out GA will often find themselves frustrated by getting stuck in local minima, diversity problem, and exuberant over-fitting. This often leads to dropping GA entirely, which is very unfortunate.

Thus, I am going to share a few neat tricks I have learnt (learned?) and implemented into my workflow successfully.

1. Randomized Environment

You know the drill. You set up the reward function (or fitness function or KPI or whatever). You pour all data you have and start GA. After a couple cups of coffee, you came back to your computer, just to find that the GA solution is grossly over-fitting that it is basically useless.

Next thing you try to do is splitting data into training set and test set. You will evolve your solutions and check against test set every X iterations. Then, you would take note of the best solution found on test set.

You thought you had this nailed. You went to have more coffee. After several hours, you came back to see that scores on test set are basically a downward line. GA learns to fit with noise in training set and the best solution on test set was from early iterations with lackluster score.

Now you are caught between a rock and a hard place.

Well, I guess 90% quits using GA because of this problem.

GA implementation works well with a noiseless data or high SNR data. But when your data is very noisy, which frankly is almost every interesting data, GA simply latches on to noise like your pet’s fur to your cloth.

After giving me headache for several weeks, I came to the solution. Instead of letting GA evaluates solutions (chromosomes) against all data, I give GA a random subset. So, the environment is now dynamic. GA will evaluate its population with 10% of data in Round 1. Then, after selection and crossover, I will create a new environment by randomly selecting a different 10% of data. GA will now have to evaluate population against this new subset. A chromosome that did well in Round 1 might get discarded in Round 2, because of the shift in environment.

I also keep track of the age of each chromosome. My fitness function will hand out a small reward for how long each chromosome has survived. Using this approach, I encourage GA to find a generalized solution.

2. Stress Testing

This one is basically an extension to the solution above. In Finance, stress testing means simulating how a financial entity, such as a bank, would perform in an economic downturn. Thus, I adopt a similar approach to GA. I would prepare a stress-test data set. This data set will be very different from the main one. Stress-test data might be mostly outliers, or simulated data with very low chance of occurrence in real life.

The idea is to see which chromosome can persist to a very stressful situation — the one you normally wouldn’t expect. Once every long while, I would throw stress-test data at GA. I would then discard most chromosomes. This is akin to an asteroid wiping out dinosaurs. It gives a breeding ground to new lifeforms and clean those who are “too used to the old way.”

3. Novelty Search

I believe it is not unusual to evolve solutions and then find out all chromosomes are pretty much the same in later iterations. The problem with this is twofold — this causes a lack of diversity in gene pool, and GA will easily get stuck in local minima. To remedy this, there is a neat trick call novelty search.

In short, instead of selecting only the best performing chromosomes, you should give some consideration as to how unique each chromosome is. There are several ways to incorporate this — giving bonus score to chromosomes with rare genes, allocating extra quota for chromosomes with unseen genes, selecting more chromosomes that have different genes than already chosen chromosomes, etc.

The key idea is that chromosomes with unique genes (parameter values) should get more consideration, because they will bring more diversity to gene pool and get you out of that local minima.

4. Carefully craft your fitness function

I think this one might be known to many, but let me emphasize — be careful of what you ask for. Many newcomers take a simplistic approach to fitness function, which can fail spectacularly.

Imagine developing a self-driving car and your reward (fitness) function is to minimize trip time. You can be sure as hell that this car is gonna drive like actors in Fast & Furious — because you forgot to include other aspects, such as traffic law infractions, passenger comfort and safety, and much more.

Take a break. Sleep on it. Well, sleep on it through many nights. Good fitness function will save you from a lot of headache.

Perhaps, you should penalize complexity in your solutions? How about giving bonus score for consistency? There are many things to ponder.
